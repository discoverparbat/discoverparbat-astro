Late October is normally the most reliable trekking weather of the year in Nepal. This week is the exception. Cyclone Arnab, which formed in the Bay of Bengal and struck India's east coast on Wednesday, has now weakened into a low-pressure system, and its moisture has been bringing rain to Nepal since Thursday evening.

The short version

Don't panic: this is now a weak system bringing long, mostly light rain, not a storm. Tonight into Saturday morning (31 Oct) brings a heavier spell in the east and centre, with heavy snow possible in the high mountains, but heavy bursts should not last more than one or two hours. Rain should stop around 4–5 pm on Saturday in most places. Sunday (1 Nov) may stay cloudy, with only a small chance of rain in the far west and around Dhorpatan and Rukum. From Monday (2 Nov) the weather looks like normal, clear November everywhere. Stay put through Saturday, take extra care near rivers, landslide slopes and avalanche paths, and keep high passes paused until the snow settles.

We are a trekking team based in Parbat District, between Pokhara and the Dhaulagiri–Annapurna mountains, and our own guides are on these trails every week. Many trekkers have contacted us worried about this storm. This update brings together what the satellites and forecast models are showing, what Nepal's Department of Hydrology and Meteorology and India's weather service have announced, the latest assessment from Nepali meteorologists, and what it all means in practice if you are trekking or about to start.

The causeWhat happened to Cyclone Arnab?

Last weekend, satellite images and forecast models showed a low-pressure system in the Bay of Bengal extending up to about 5.8 km above sea level, sitting over sea surface temperatures of around 29°C with low vertical wind shear, all conditions that favour a storm getting stronger. India's weather services forecast it to become a cyclone early this week, and it took the name Arnab, a Bengali word for the ocean.

On Wednesday afternoon (28 October), the storm's wind speed was measured at about 125 km/h near Visakhapatnam in Andhra Pradesh. That is a powerful storm for India's coast, and some Indian media coverage has understandably sounded alarming. For Nepal, though, the picture is very different.

The cyclone is roughly 2,000 km from Nepal. As it moves inland over India it loses the warm sea that feeds it and weakens quickly, so by the time its moisture reaches the Himalaya the destructive cyclone winds will be gone. What does reach Nepal is a large amount of moisture. A westerly trough in the upper atmosphere is expected to help pull it north, and Nepal's steep terrain squeezes extra rain and snow out of the air as it rises.

That is exactly what happened. Since then, Arnab has lost its cyclone structure and weakened into a low-pressure system. For Nepal it has been a rain-and-snow event, not a windstorm, and the right response remains to be careful, not to panic.

What happenedThe storm took the weaker path (Scenario 2)

Earlier this week, Nepali meteorologists described two possible paths for the storm. It has now taken the second: it weakened into a low-pressure system. Here are both, for reference.

Scenario 1: it stays a cyclone for longer

Did not happen

The storm keeps its cyclone structure further inland. Rain and high-altitude snow in Nepal are heavier, peaking on Friday, but the system also moves through faster, with conditions easing on Saturday and improving from around Sunday 1 November.

Scenario 2: it weakens into a low-pressure system

This is happening

By the time it reaches central Chhattisgarh, the cyclone may weaken into a low-pressure system. That would reduce both wind and rain intensity, which is good news. The trade-off is that a weaker system can linger over Nepal for longer, meaning four to five days of overcast skies and steady light rain, likely into Monday 2 or Tuesday 3 November.

For trekkers, this is the gentler outcome: less intense rain, smaller flood risk and no strong winds. The cost is time. Light rain has been falling for longer, views will stay hidden until Sunday afternoon at the earliest, and trails will stay wet for a while. The better news is that the clearance now looks sooner than the four-to-five-day worst case: rain should stop on Saturday afternoon, and Monday looks like normal November weather.

ImpactHow much rain and snow to expect

Rain so far, and tonight

A Nepali meteorologist's update at 5 pm on Friday (Kartik 13) showed rain across the whole country, mostly light but long-lasting since the system weakened. Average rainfall over the previous 12 hours:

Koshi Provinceabout 40 mm
Madhesh Provinceabout 20 mm
Bagmati Provinceabout 15 mm

The rain has been moving gradually from the east towards Bagmati and Gandaki. From this evening until Saturday morning, it is expected to become somewhat more intense across Koshi, Madhesh, Bagmati and Gandaki, with heavy rain possible in some places and heavy snow possible in the high Himalaya. The reassuring part: even where heavy rain falls, it is not expected to last more than one or two hours, and most of the time it will be drizzle, so the chance of a major disaster is low. Even so, this kind of rain can still trigger sudden local landslides, flooding and avalanches, so take particular care in areas known for them.

Earlier forecasts

Earlier forecasts suggested that many hill areas could receive around 100–200 mm of rain over the storm period. Nepal's Department of Hydrology and Meteorology expects moderate rain across Koshi, Madhesh, Bagmati, Gandaki and Lumbini provinces, with heavy rain in a few places in Koshi, Bagmati and Gandaki. Meteorologist Binod Pokharel has identified 29 districts at higher risk, mostly in Lumbini, Gandaki, Bagmati and Koshi, with parts of Bagmati possibly topping 150 mm. Karnali and Sudurpaschim in the far west are expected to see less.

With the system now weakened, totals so far are well below those early worst-case numbers, with the rain spread more thinly over a longer period.

For trekkers, rain on this scale means swollen streams, slippery stone steps, leeches returning to lower forests, and a real risk of landslides on mountain roads.

Snow in the high Himalaya

Above roughly 4,000 m, the same moisture falls as snow. Earlier model runs showed snowfall of 0.25 to 1.5 metres possible in the high mountains, with the heaviest falls most likely around the Dhaulagiri, Annapurna and Manaslu ranges. Heavy snow is also possible in parts of Koshi and Bagmati, and lighter snow in the mountains of the far west. The weaker system should mean lower totals, but tonight's heavier spell can still bring a substantial fresh fall, and even moderate fresh snow on high passes is enough to make them dangerous.

Lower ridges are not entirely out of reach. On the highest points in the 3,000–3,300 m range, including Poon Hill and Kokhe Danda, sleet or a light covering of snow is possible during the coldest part of the storm. Temperatures across the country are expected to drop, so nights will feel noticeably colder through the weekend.

Why we still take this seriously: almost exactly a year ago, the remnants of Cyclone Montha brought heavy snow to Nepal's mountains and contributed to a deadly early-November snowstorm on two Himalayan peaks. Wind is not the danger in Nepal. Deep fresh snow and avalanches at altitude, and floods and landslides lower down, are.

Your trekWhat it means for each type of trek

The risk depends mostly on how high you are going and whether your route crosses a pass or an avalanche-prone valley. Here is how we are thinking about our own groups and the main routes around us.

High passes and high-altitude circuits

Paused until at least Mon 2 Nov

Annapurna Circuit over Thorong La (5,416 m), Manaslu Circuit over Larkya La (5,106 m), the Dhaulagiri Circuit over French Pass, Tilicho Lake, Mesokanto La and Khayer Lake (4,653 m) on the Khopra route.

These are the routes in the heaviest snow zone, and tonight's heavier spell may add more fresh snow. Do not plan any crossing before Monday 2 November at the earliest. Even after the skies clear, passes can stay closed or unsafe for several days while snow settles and a trail is broken. Wait for local confirmation before going up.

Annapurna Base Camp and Mardi Himal

Pause high sections

The stretch of the ABC trail between Deurali and Machhapuchhre Base Camp runs through a narrow valley that is exposed to avalanches after heavy snow. The upper camps on Mardi Himal are similarly exposed to wind and snow.

Stay at Chhomrong, Sinuwa or Bamboo on ABC, or below Forest Camp on Mardi Himal, until the storm has passed and lodge owners confirm the trail is safe.

Mid-altitude ridge treks

Restart Sun 1 or Mon 2 Nov

Poon Hill, Khopra Ridge Trek (3,667 m), the Muldai Viewpoint trek and the Kokhe Danda Trek (3,309 m).

These routes stay mostly below the deep snow line, so the danger is lower. But rain, cloud and possible sleet on the ridges mean wet, slippery trails, no mountain views and cold nights. Sunrise viewpoints are clouded in until at least Sunday afternoon. We are holding our own departures through Saturday. Walking can restart with care on Sunday 1 November, but Monday 2 November is the better start day if you want the views.

Lower hills and village walks

Possible with care

Short walks around Pokhara, Parbat's villages and homestays at lower elevations.

Fine on lighter days, but avoid river crossings, steep slopes above roads and long drives on mountain roads during tonight's heavier rain and Saturday morning. From Saturday evening these are fine again.

Already trekkingIf you are on the trail right now

The heavier spell is tonight and Saturday morning. If you are on the trail, the plan is simple: stay where you are, warm and dry, and let the weather pass.

  • Stay put through Saturday: in a well-established teahouse village at a sensible altitude, away from avalanche paths and river banks. Do not start a pass crossing, glacier section or high camp until the snow has settled and locals give the go-ahead.
  • Plan to wait, not push: lower routes can move again on Sunday; for high routes, think Monday or later. A rest day in a warm dining room is far better than a crossing in fresh snow.
  • Listen to locals: teahouse owners, guides and local authorities know which slopes slide and which bridges flood. If they say wait, wait.
  • Stay in contact: tell your family, agency or insurer where you are and what your plan is. Charge phones and power banks while electricity is available.
  • Keep warm and dry: carry a waterproof jacket, warm layers, dry socks and a cover for your pack. Temperatures will drop.
  • Check insurance: make sure your policy covers helicopter rescue at the altitude you are at, and keep the emergency number handy. Note that rescue flights are often impossible during the storm itself.

Arriving soonIf your trek starts this week

If you land in Kathmandu or Pokhara around now, the simplest plan is to start walking on Monday 2 November, or on Sunday for lower routes if you don't mind cloud. Spend Saturday in Pokhara or Kathmandu, or on a short cultural visit at low altitude. For high passes, keep a few extra days in hand after Monday for the snow to settle.

Do not start anything tonight or on Saturday that would put you high in the mountains or on a long mountain road.

Lodges along the popular routes will also be juggling cancellations and stranded trekkers this week, so it helps to call ahead once you have new dates. Our guide on booking teahouses in advance in Nepal explains how.

Getting aroundRoads and flights

Avoid all non-essential long-distance road travel tonight and on Saturday until the rain stops in the afternoon, and stay alert around rivers and known landslide areas. Extra care is needed on the Prithvi Highway between Kathmandu and Pokhara and on the BP Highway, where heavy rain can bring down landslides. Mountain roads to trailheads, including Beni to Jomsom, Besisahar to Manang and the roads towards Banthati, Nayapul and Kokhe Danda's starting points, may be blocked for some time. Even after the rain stops, check road conditions before setting off on Sunday, as landslide clearance can take a day or two.

Mountain flights such as Pokhara to Jomsom and Kathmandu to Lukla depend on clear visibility and are likely to be delayed or cancelled while the clouds sit low. Flights should resume as the cloud lifts on Sunday and Monday. Expect a backlog, so keep a buffer of at least one or two days before any international flight home.

The upsideAfter the storm: what to expect

Rain should stop on Saturday afternoon, cloud should break up by Sunday afternoon, and from Monday 2 November the weather looks like normal, clear November across the country. In autumn, the days after a big storm are often the clearest of the season. The air is washed clean of dust and haze, and freshly snow-covered peaks look spectacular from viewpoints like Poon Hill, Khopra Ridge and Kokhe Danda.

That makes early November a very good time for ridge treks with views. For high passes, though, give it time. Deep fresh snow brings avalanche risk and makes route-finding hard until it settles and a track is broken, and some routes may stay closed for a few days. Ask locally before you go high, and do not be the first group to test a pass after heavy snowfall.

Forecasts for tropical cyclones change as they develop, so this page was a planning guide rather than a guarantee. The storm has now passed. FAQFrequently asked questions

When will Cyclone Arnab affect Nepal?

It already is. Rain began on Thursday evening, 29 October, and by Friday 30 October (Kartik 13) it was raining across the country. Arnab has weakened into a low-pressure system, so most of the rain is light but long-lasting. It is expected to pick up tonight into Saturday morning, ease through Saturday and stop around Saturday afternoon in most places.

Will there be strong winds in Nepal from Cyclone Arnab?

No. The storm was measured at about 125 km/h near Visakhapatnam in Andhra Pradesh on 28 October, but it has since weakened into a low-pressure system. In Nepal the effects are rain in the hills and snow in the high mountains, not wind.

How much rain and snow is expected in Nepal?

In the 12 hours to 5 pm on Friday 30 October, average rainfall was about 40 mm in Koshi, 20 mm in Madhesh, 15 mm in Bagmati and 7 mm in Gandaki, and the rain is moving westward. Heavier bursts are possible tonight into Saturday morning in Koshi, Madhesh, Bagmati and Gandaki, but they should not last more than one or two hours. Heavy snow is possible in the high Himalaya.

Is it safe to trek in Nepal during Cyclone Arnab?

There is no need to panic, but stay put until the rain stops. High-altitude treks and pass crossings should stay paused until at least Monday 2 November and local confirmation that trails are open. Lower and mid-altitude routes can restart on Sunday with care, or better on Monday when clear November weather is expected.

Should I cross Thorong La or Larkya La this week?

Not before Monday 2 November at the earliest, and only after local teams confirm the trail has been opened and the fresh snow has settled. Deep new snow brings avalanche and route-finding risks for several days after a storm.

What should I do if I am already on a high trek?

Stay in a safe teahouse village through Saturday and wait for clearer weather on Sunday and Monday. Do not start a pass crossing, glacier section or avalanche-prone stretch until the snow has settled and locals say it is safe. Keep your guide, teahouse owner and family informed of your plans.

When will the weather clear after Cyclone Arnab?

Rain should stop in most places by around 4 to 5 pm on Saturday 31 October. Cloud may linger until Sunday afternoon, with only a small chance of rain on Sunday in the far west and around Dhorpatan and Rukum. From Monday 2 November, typical clear November weather is expected across Nepal.

Will Poon Hill and Kokhe Danda get snow?

The highest ridges in the 3,000–3,300 m range, including Poon Hill and Kokhe Danda, could see sleet or a light dusting of snow during the coldest part of the storm tonight. The main concern on these routes is rain, slippery stone steps and cold. Views should return from Monday 2 November.
